01 · BriefThe real problem

The brief was a service model. The real problem was that "success" meant four different things to four teams.

Autodesk needed its post-purchase experience to do more than retain customers — it needed to generate new revenue. The existing model was fragmented: three teams (Sales, Success, Support) touching the same accounts with different goals, different tools, and no shared definition of what a successful customer was.

Product measured success in feature adoption. Success measured it in QBR completion. Support saw it as ticket deflection. Sales measured it in renewal probability. All four were right. None of them were talking to each other.

02 · TradeoffsThe essential calls

Three tradeoffs had to be named before any design could hold.

Before rebuilding the journey, I surfaced three tradeoffs the organization had been navigating implicitly — and made the calls explicit. Naming these was the design work; the tier model and the blueprint were the consequences.

Tradeoff 01

Differentiated experience vs. scalable delivery

Enterprise accounts needed 1:few executive engagement. The long tail needed digital-guided equivalents. The tier model let the same journey run at two delivery intensities — Growth Plus for ~700–1000 accounts, Nurture Plus for ~400–600 — without bifurcating the operating model.

Call — One journey. Two tiers. Same backstage.
Tradeoff 02

Ambition vs. organizational capacity

My first design included Success Planning workshops as a standard deliverable on every account. Internal validation killed it: CSMs were already overextended on at-risk accounts. Shipping a tier that required capacity we didn't have would have broken both the model and the team.

Call — Success Planning became a hand-raiser activation, not a default. The tier performed better because the team could actually deliver what it promised.
Tradeoff 03

Service catalog vs. outcome journey

A catalog of services is easy to sell and hard to deliver consistently. An outcome-shaped journey — Identify · Evaluate · Create · Execute · Assess — is harder to sell but easier to deliver and measure. We built the journey first, then mapped services into phases as activations.

Call — Journey-shaped catalog. Every offering tied to a phase and an outcome.

What made it ship

Sales, Success, and Partner teams could point at the same phase and mean the same thing. Before the blueprint, "success planning" happened in three different phases depending on who said it. After, it lived in Phase 03 — Create a plan — with named owners, platform entry points, and handoff criteria.

04 · ModernizationOne operating system

The design work that mattered most was invisible to the customer.

A journey blueprint is a slide until it's wired into the systems the teams actually work in. The second half of this engagement was driving the tech modernization that let Sales and Success run off the same operating model — not the same meeting.

BEFORE

Handoffs through email and slides

  • CSM plans in Gainsight · AE plans in SFDC
  • Customer health lived in one tool, renewal signal in another
  • Tier logic existed in a slide deck
  • QBRs synthesized manually before each meeting
→
AFTER

Tier-aware playbooks firing in-system

  • Gainsight — journey-phase playbooks, health scores wired to renewal signal, outcome tracking
  • Salesforce — tier logic on the account record, upsell triggers fed from adoption data
  • Shared telemetry · one customer record · both teams see the same moment
  • QBR deck assembles itself from platform state

Hardest part wasn't the configuration — it was getting two teams with different quotas to agree that a single shared signal was more valuable than two separate ones they each controlled. The blueprint gave them a shared object to stand around. The platform change made the shared object operational.

06 · LessonWhat I'd do differently
Service design that doesn't model organizational capacity isn't service design. It's customer-journey design with a larger font.

The work that mattered most wasn't the five phases or the three tradeoffs. It was the discipline of forcing the operating model — people, process, platform — to carry the same logic the blueprint drew on paper. Without that second half, the journey would have been a deck. With it, the journey became how Sales and Success shipped.

Next time I'd do the capacity audit before the design, not after internal validation caught the gap. The tradeoffs chapter you just read existed in my head from week one. Writing them down as a contract — before the design — would have saved the program six weeks of rework.
