01 · BriefThe problem

Position me as a design leader, not a practitioner.

March 6, 2026. I needed a portfolio that read as leadership — strategy, system architecture, tradeoffs — not as a gallery of artifacts. The traditional path (hire a dev, buy a template, wait six weeks) wasn't viable. The tools had just crossed a threshold where building it myself was genuinely possible.

What followed was six weeks of structured human-AI collaboration that produced this site, two workflows I'll use for years, and one sharp lesson about where AI ends and judgment begins.

02 · ToolchainWhat each tool did

Four tools, four lanes. No overlap.

Build environment

Cursor

Where code lives, gets written, gets applied. Most reliable given structured, specific instructions. Makes poor design decisions on its own.

Thinking partner

Claude

Strategy, copy, critique, structured prompts. Every draft, persona, QA run went through it first. It generated. I decided.

Visual prototyping

Claude Design

Launched Apr 17, six weeks in. Moved visual work out of code and into conversation. The dashboard on Impact was designed here, implemented in Cursor.

Deploy

Vercel

Instant previews on every push. This matters more than it sounds — real output in a real browser every few minutes is what makes tight iteration loops possible.

03 · MethodCentaur, not Cyborg

Human in the saddle. Always.

Ethan Mollick's framework distinguishes Centaurs — humans and AI dividing tasks, human steering — from Cyborgs, where the integration is total and inseparable. I'm a Centaur. The structure of every page, the positioning decisions, the copy that sounds like me — those were mine. AI produced faster drafts, better variations, sharper critique than I could generate alone. The decisions were mine.

AI generated

"Matt Stangl is a strategic design leader who transforms complex enterprise challenges into measurable business outcomes through human-centered methodologies and systems thinking."

I changed it to

"I rebuild fragmented enterprise experience as one operating model that actually ships."

Why — The AI version is a job description. Mine is a positioning statement. AI defaults to comprehensiveness; I default to specificity. That pattern repeated across every piece of copy on this site.

05 · Failure modeWhat I got wrong

I skipped the brief twice. I paid for it twice.

First time: I asked Cursor to "design the impact dashboard" without specifying hierarchy, metric priority, or interaction logic. It produced something technically correct and visually incoherent — every element equally weighted because I hadn't said what mattered most. Two hours fixing what a 15-minute brief would have prevented.

Second time: I let AI write three case study pages end to end. The output was fluent, professional, and completely generic — the same portfolio language I'd seen on a hundred other sites. I deleted it and started over with the real stories, the real pivots, the real metrics. That version is what you're reading now.

The tell

The ratio of presentation quality to case study depth is the AI red flag — not the AI use itself. Experienced reviewers catch it in 30 seconds. The fix was never less AI. It was more specificity.

07 · LessonWhat I'd do differently

What I'd hand to someone starting tomorrow.

Specificity beats creativity in prompts. "Make this better" gets noise. "This reads as a practitioner, not a leader — rewrite the first sentence to lead with strategic scope" gets signal.

The tools don't know what you're trying to say. AI is fluent but not opinionated. Every piece of copy it generates needs a human judgment call about whether it sounds like you.

The pace of change is disorienting. The workflow I had on March 6 is different from the one I have now. Claude Design didn't exist six weeks ago. Stay loose — the tool you're waiting for might ship next week.

AI raises the floor, not the ceiling. It makes bad work good fast. It doesn't make good work great — that still takes taste, judgment, and knowing what you're trying to say.
